# Personas — 2026-08-06

Four readers the public pages must satisfy. Each entry records who the
reader is, what they test before they trust, and what would astonish them.
The methodology and about rewrites are judged against these four.

The shared standard the four impose, stated once: **a reader must be able to
reproduce the work.** Not admire it — repeat it. Every threshold, gate, cut,
and floor is named with its value; every count is computed from the corpus at
build time; every failure that set a rule is printed next to the rule. What
cannot be reproduced from the page is not claimed on it.

## P1 — Mr. Smith, Springfield, Illinois

Solo practitioner, admitted 1988. General practice: estates, real
property, family, occasional personal injury. Learned research on the
Illinois Digest in print; moved to Westlaw and never left. Pays for it,
resents the invoice, trusts it anyway — because attorney-editors wrote
the headnotes, the Key Numbers never move, and KeyCite tells him when a
case dies. He has watched free legal websites for thirty years and
concluded they are written by no one, checked by no one, and abandoned
within five.

What he tests:

- Opens a digest in a field he knows and reads for error. One wrong
  statement of Illinois or federal doctrine ends the visit.
- Follows a citation. If the authority is not there, in full, at the
  same address, the site is "another blog."
- Looks for a person. An unsigned reference work is an anonymous letter.
- Reads the tone. A law book states; it does not sell. Any trace of
  startup language — "revolutionary," "AI-powered," an exclamation
  point — confirms his prior.

What astonishes him: the audit trail. Westlaw never showed him what the
editors rejected. A digest that publishes its discards — and a project
that deleted work below its own evidence floor instead of shipping it —
reads as editorial spine, the thing he thought died with the print
digests.

## P2 — Ms. Barbara Goldenberg, law librarian, Columbia

MLIS and JD; twenty-five years in academic law libraries; teaches
advanced legal research. Holds that classification is power, that the
profession handed that power to one vendor in the 1890s, and that SKOS
is not hard — prefLabel, broader, narrower, a first-year student can
read it — people only believe otherwise because the vendor's apparatus
is the water they swim in. She reviews vocabularies for sport and is
merciless about the usual sins.

What she tests, before reading any homepage prose:

- Fetches the concept scheme and validates it. One prefLabel per
  language; pref, alt, and hidden labels disjoint; no label doing
  identity work.
- Checks the IRIs. Path-derived identifiers churn on reorganization;
  she has watched vocabularies destroy themselves this way. Opaque,
  minted, never reused, with the old routes kept as history — or it is
  a toy.
- Reads the mapping properties. exactMatch inflation is the tell of a
  vocabulary that wants credit it has not earned. closeMatch honestly
  used earns more.
- Looks for the profile: documented constraints, published shapes, and
  a plain statement of what the vocabulary does not assert.

What astonishes her: a public project that made the standard mistake —
path IRIs — and then documented the mistake, minted opaque identifiers,
kept every legacy route in an append-only registry, and published the
shapes that now enforce the rules. That is the correction the textbooks
prescribe and almost no one performs in public. It is also her argument,
running in production: SKOS, no vendor, readable by anyone.

## P3 — Mr. Rodrigues, foreign-trained attorney, New York

Trained in a civil-law jurisdiction; LLM at Michigan; New York bar;
cross-border corporate practice at a Manhattan firm. His legal mind was
formed by codes: a system stated once, in order, general part before
special part — find the article, apply the article. American doctrine
feels to him like a building with no plan: the rule exists, everyone
cites it, and it is written down nowhere — it _is_ a line of cases, and
his colleagues connect those dots by an instinct no one can explain to
him. He uses Westlaw to learn what is normal, headnotes standing in for
the code he misses.

What he tests:

- Is there a map? Area, topic, issue, in order, with definitions and
  scope notes — the systematic statement his training expects and
  American law refuses to provide.
- Does a digest show its skeleton? Not prose that assumes the reader
  already knows which cases matter, but the authorities listed, in
  full, with the digest built visibly on top of them.
- Does anything explain the practice itself — why there is no code,
  and how the profession organizes doctrine without one?

What astonishes him: learning that America already answered his
question once — the digest tradition classified the whole case law
precisely because no code would — and that this project is that answer
rebuilt in the open. The connecting-the-dots practice stops being tribal
knowledge; the dots and the connections are on the same page. He starts
structuring memos with it.

## P4 — Ms. Erica Yong, senior corporate counsel, OpenAI

Former BigLaw technology-transactions partner-track associate; now
senior counsel. Reads everything as diligence and every project as a
potential move. Her current problem: a competitor's legal product is
taking mindshare, and she wants to walk into a room with the
countermove — OpenAI sponsors the open infrastructure of American
legal doctrine. Cheap relative to product spend, unanswerable as a
narrative, and it grounds models in licensed, provenance-complete legal
text — the exact input legal AI lacks.

What she tests:

- Licensing, in one read: CC BY digests, public-domain or as-licensed
  sources, a stated legal theory for republication. Anything murky is
  a non-starter.
- Provenance, machine-readable: per-bundle manifests, model chains
  disclosed, audits published. That is what makes the corpus usable
  for grounding rather than a liability.
- Overclaiming, anywhere. One inflated claim and the project becomes a
  reputational risk she will not put her name near.
- Governance with a surface to fund: named maintainer, named gates,
  and blockers that money and people convert into milestones.

What astonishes her: the project states plainly that no validated-
quality claim is available until an independent qualified reviewer
exists. A disclosure most projects would bury is, to her, the entire
pitch: the blocking gates are line items, the honesty is the brand, and
sponsorship buys the unblocking — credited on every page of the first
open, provenance-complete map of American law.
